Introduction

Reading past the totals

The dashboard shows the national picture clearly: 51,022 facilities, 68% publicly owned, and a system dominated by primary care (88%). What it doesn't show, because a single-page dashboard can't without becoming unreadable, is how differently that national picture plays out zone by zone. This report exists to fill that gap: five findings drawn from the same underlying dataset, each pointing at a pattern the totals alone would hide.

Finding 01

Ownership isn't one national pattern, it's two different countries

The national split (68% public, 23% private) is an average of two very different regional realities.

Ownership breakdown by zone (%)
ZonePublicPrivateUnknown
North West89.7%7.9%2.5%
North East84.5%9.1%6.4%
North Central70.8%21.8%7.4%
South South65.3%17.5%17.1%
South East53.7%32.6%13.7%
South West46.3%42.9%10.8%

At the state level, the divide is even sharper than the zone averages suggest. Lagos runs 70.7% private, 22.4% public, which is almost a mirror image of the North West zone's 89.7% public share. A policy conversation about "encouraging more private investment in care" would land completely differently in Lagos, where private providers already dominate, than in Sokoto or Katsina, where the system is almost entirely government-run.

Why this matters: any recommendation tied to ownership structure should be scoped to a zone or state, not stated nationally. The 68/23/9 split is real, but it's an average masking a genuine north-south structural difference in how care is delivered and financed.

Kano ranks 10th nationally by raw facility count (1,723) but drops to near the bottom on density (39.2 per LGA) because it administers 44 LGAs — the most of any state. Borno is the opposite kind of concern: it's low on both raw count (804, second-lowest nationally) and density (28.7), which is the stronger indicator of genuine under-coverage rather than an artifact of how the state's LGAs are drawn.
Finding 03

Referral-level care is concentrated almost entirely in the south

Tertiary facilities (teaching and specialized hospitals) are the rarest tier nationally at just 506 of 51,022 facilities (1.0%). Where they exist matters more than how many.

Tertiary facilities by zone
ZoneTertiary facilitiesShare of national total
South West17534.6%
South East15230.0%
South South7314.4%
North Central5210.3%
North West407.9%
North East142.8%

The South West and South East together account for 64.6% of the country's tertiary facilities, led by Anambra (59) and Lagos (56). The North East, by contrast, has only 14 across its entire zone, despite serving 6,857 total facilities' worth of primary-level demand. For a patient in the North East needing specialist or teaching-hospital-level care, the nearest option is structurally far more likely to require crossing state or zone lines than it would for someone in Lagos or Anambra.

Finding 04

Primary-care reliance and the tertiary gap move together

The North's heavier reliance on primary-tier facilities isn't a separate observation from Finding 03. The two are directly connected.

Facility tier composition by zone (%)
ZonePrimarySecondaryTertiary
North East94.4%4.3%0.2%
North West93.6%4.7%0.4%
North Central92.2%7.3%0.5%
South South84.9%13.9%1.3%

South East81.0%17.0%2.0%

Northern zones run 92–94% primary-tier facilities; southern zones run 81–85%. That roughly 10-point gap is exactly where the "missing" secondary and tertiary capacity in the North goes — it isn't distributed differently, it's largely absent. This is consistent with, and helps explain, the tertiary concentration in Finding 03.

Finding 05

The registry itself has a record-keeping gap worth naming

"Public" ownership is well recorded at the top level, but the finer detail of which government tier runs each facility is missing for over a third of all records.

At 38.3% unrecorded, "Unknown" is nearly as large as the biggest real category (Local Government, 42.6%). This gap isn't evenly spread — it concentrates in specific states: Ogun (370 unknown records), Niger (355), Imo (347), Anambra (310), and Delta (290) account for a disproportionate share of the missing data nationally.

Why this matters: "Unknown" does not read as "no government presence." It's a data-collection gap, not evidence of privatization or absence.
Summary

Key takeaways

1

Ownership splits regionally, not nationally. The South West (and Lagos especially) leans private; the North West is almost entirely public. A single national ownership statistic understates this divide.

2

Density, not just raw count, flags real coverage gaps. Borno is thin by both measures; Kano looks strong by count but weak by density once its 44 LGAs are accounted for.

3

Referral-level care concentrates in the south. 64.6% of all tertiary facilities sit in the South West and South East; the North East has just 14 for its entire zone.

4

The primary-care skew in the North directly mirrors the tertiary-care gap — roughly 10 percentage points of "missing" secondary/tertiary capacity shows up as extra primary-tier reliance instead.

5

38.3% of records lack an ownership sub-type. A genuine data-quality finding, not a silent gap, as stated in my methodology.
