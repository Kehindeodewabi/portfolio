01 — Objective

Where does a profitable store quietly lose money?

On paper, Superstore is in good shape: $2.30M in sales and a 12.5% margin. But one overall margin can hide a lot. Some products sell well and earn almost nothing, and some regions work harder for less.

This project works down from the totals to find what is responsible, and what the store could change. It uses four years of order data and Python, and every figure on this page comes from a named section of one notebook.

  1. Which categories and products drive profit, and which lose money?
  2. What separates the profitable parts of the business from the unprofitable ones?
  3. Where, and when, should the business act to protect its profit?

02 — Data

9,994 order lines, four years, one question

The dataset is the Superstore sales file: 21 columns covering orders, customers, locations, products, sales, quantity, discount and profit. Each row is one order line, meaning one product on one order. A customer who buys a chair, a table and paper in one purchase creates one order and three order lines.

9,994order lines5,009orders793customers49states

The file has no duplicate rows and no missing values. The two date columns were stored as text, so they were converted to real dates (day first) before any year or month analysis. There is no cost column: the profit figure is already sales minus cost, so margin is profit divided by sales.

03 — Method

One pattern, repeated: group, calculate, print, plot

Nearly every section of the notebook splits the data into groups, calculates a measure for each group, prints the table and draws a chart with the values written on the bars. That keeps each finding easy to check. A few short snippets from the notebook appear through this page.

  1. Clean and check

    Load the CSV, convert both date columns, then check for duplicates, missing values and data types.

  2. Group and measure

    Total sales, total profit and margin by category, sub-category, region, state and customer segment.

  3. Test the discounts

    Average profit at each discount level, discounts grouped into bands, and the correlation between discount and profit.

  4. Isolate the losses

    Pull out every loss-making line, trace the losses to sub-categories, then examine Tables alone by discount, region and year.

  5. Check the clock and the customer

    Yearly and monthly trends, then segment and shipping mode, to rule things in or out.

04 — Key Findings

Furniture sells like the others and earns almost nothing

Finding 1 · Category

A 2.5% margin against 17% for everything else

Furniture brings in $742K, 32% of all sales, but only 6% of profit. Technology and Office Supplies earn margins of 17.4% and 17.0%. The next step was to look inside Furniture.

Finding 2 · Sub-category

Three sub-categories lose money, and Tables lose the most

Tables lose $17.7K on $207K of sales. Bookcases (-$3.5K) and Supplies (-$1.2K) also lose, and Machines barely break even at a 1.8% margin. Copiers earn the most per line ($818), but from only 68 lines, so they are not a typical order.

Finding 3 · Discounts

Past a 20% discount, the store starts losing money

Average profit per order line is positive from 0% to 20% and negative from 30% upward. Grouped into bands, lines with no discount earned $321K. The three bands above 20% lost a combined $135K, and every line above 40% lost money. Discount and profit have a correlation of -0.22: a moderate link, not proof of cause.

Finding 4 · Loss-making lines

One order line in five loses money, and the losses are not only Tables

1,871 lines (18.7%) lose money, a combined $156K. That wipes out over a third of the $443K that profitable lines earn. Binders (-$38.5K), Tables (-$32.4K) and Machines (-$30.1K) lose the most on their loss-making lines. Binders stay profitable overall because their winning lines outweigh the losers; Tables do not.

The sub-categories that discount most tell the same story. Machines, Tables and Bookcases are three of the four most-discounted sub-categories and three of the four lowest-margin ones. Binders, discounted most of all, is the exception because of its volume.

06 — Where

The weakest places are the ones that discount most

The West earns a 14.9% margin on an 11% average discount. Central earns 7.9% on 24%. Furniture in Central lost $2.9K, the only region and category pair to lose money.

At state level, 10 of 49 states lose money. Texas (-$25.7K), Ohio (-$17.0K), Pennsylvania (-$15.6K) and Illinois (-$12.6K) lose $70.9K between them, at average discounts of 32% to 39%. The five most profitable states average only 0% to 7%.

07 — When

Profit grew every year, but the best months are not the busiest

Sales dipped 2.8% in 2012, then grew 29.3% and 20.6%. Profit rose every year, from $49.5K to $93.5K. Technology drove the growth ($21.5K to $50.7K); Furniture stayed small and uneven ($5.5K, $3.0K, $7.0K, $3.0K).

Sales peak in September, November and December. But November, the busiest month at $349K, earns only a 10.3% margin, while February, the quietest, earns the best (17.1%). The average discount is steady at 15% to 17% all year, so heavier discounting does not explain it. Product mix is a more likely cause and worth checking.

08 — Ruled Out

Customer segment is not the problem

Margins are close across the three segments: Home Office earns 14.0%, Corporate 13.0% and Consumer 11.5%. Shipping mode makes little difference either, with margins of 12% to 14% across all four modes. Whatever is leaking profit is not about who buys or how it ships.

09 — Recommendations

What the data supports doing

  1. Cap discounts at 20%

    Average profit is positive up to 20% and negative from 30%. Start with Tables, where capped lines earned +$13.0K and the rest lost $30.7K.

  2. Review Central and East discount approvals

    Central averages a 24% discount and the lowest margin (7.9%). East Tables averaged 37% and lost $11.0K.

  3. Reprice Tables, Binders and Machines

    The largest losses on loss-making lines: $32.4K, $38.5K and $30.1K. Binders average a 37% discount and Machines 31%.

  4. Audit Texas, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Illinois

    A combined loss of $70.9K at average discounts of 32% to 39%, against 0% to 7% in the most profitable states.

10 — Limitations

What this analysis cannot say

  • Association, not cause. Heavy discounts go with losses, but the data cannot prove they cause them. Discounts may be given on higher-cost or slower-moving items.
  • No cost data. A discount cap might reduce sales volume. The deep-discounted Tables lines brought in $90K and lost $30.7K, so profit could still rise, but that should be tested before it is assumed.
  • Small groups. Some discount levels have few lines (11 at 45%; 36 for Tables at 50%), so single bars are indicative.
  • Region and state gaps may partly reflect product mix, not only discount policy.
  • Four years of history. Patterns may have changed since 2014.

The sensible next step is a controlled test: cap discounts at 20% in one region for a quarter and compare margin and sales against a similar region.
